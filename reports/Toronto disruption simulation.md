# Build Toronto disruption models before predictions

**Use a road-network model to calculate disruption effects, and AI to prepare scenarios and explain results.** Toronto offers useful road geometry, reported restrictions, historical traffic counts and TTC data, but these do not supply a complete picture of current travel demand. For this three-person, downtown-first hackathon project, the achievable first result is a credible closure-and-detour comparison on the existing Mapbox map. Add approximate congestion only after the baseline routes work, using visible demand and capacity assumptions. Events change demand as well as street access; accidents temporarily change capacity, speed and permissions. Neither drawing traffic nor asking an LLM for plausible delays constitutes a validated simulation. The recommendations below distinguish documented capabilities, observations from the research probes on October 17, 2026, and proposed implementation choices.

## Toronto data supplies the backbone, with gaps

The existing display extract should become a **connected, directed road graph** before it becomes a traffic engine. Toronto Centreline v2 includes persistent centreline and intersection IDs, direction fields and road classification. The inspected schema did not include lane capacities or legal turn restrictions. Use intersection IDs to establish connectivity and filter drivable classes; connecting every geometric crossing would introduce false turns at bridges and underpasses. The live catalogue reported daily publication, but each input still needs its own observation date. ([Toronto Centreline](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/toronto-centreline-tcl/))

| Input | Practical use | Material limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Toronto Centreline](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/toronto-centreline-tcl/) | Road IDs, geometry, junction connectivity and direction | Needs routing validation and enrichment for turns, lanes, speeds and capacity |
| [Road restrictions](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/road-restrictions/) | Reported planned restrictions, closures and RESCU incidents | Applicant-submitted conditions are not field-verified; restrictions are not necessarily full closures |
| [Intersection turning counts](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/traffic-volumes-at-intersections-for-all-modes/) | Fifteen-minute vehicle movements, plus pedestrian and bicycle counts | Ad hoc observations across different dates, rather than continuous citywide demand |
| [Midblock speed and volume counts](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/traffic-volumes-midblock-vehicle-speed-volume-and-classification-counts/) | Directional traffic volumes and sampled speed distributions | Usually short surveys at individual sites; historical observations are not live congestion |
| [Merged TTC GTFS](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/merged-gtfs-ttc-routes-and-schedules/) and [surface real-time feed](https://bustime.ttc.ca/gtfsrt) | Routes, stops, schedules, vehicle positions and trip updates | Surface real-time IDs require the matching BusTime static feed; passenger demand remains missing |
| [Festivals & Events](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/festivals-events/) | Candidate event locations, dates and descriptions | Organizer-submitted calendar is incomplete and does not guarantee attendance or travel profiles |

Feed ingestion needs validation and a last-valid cache. The restrictions endpoint returned HTTP 200 during research, but a full JSON parse failed on an invalid escape. This is an observed response problem, not evidence of a permanent outage; test CSV/XML alternatives and expose snapshot age. Match restrictions to road segments using geometry, names and direction, and retain ambiguous matches for review. ([Restrictions feed](https://secure.toronto.ca/opendata/cart/road_restrictions/v3?format=json))

**Do not assume a free operational City travel-time feed exists.** The City’s Bluetooth catalogue describes real-time data, but its transportation data documentation says the real-time feed is not operational; inspected resources were historical archives. Historical serious/fatal collision data is useful safety context, with delayed finalization, rather than live accident detection or blockage duration. ([City transportation data documentation](https://github.com/CityofToronto/bdit_data-sources#open-data-releases), [KSI collision dataset](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/motor-vehicle-collisions-involving-killed-or-seriously-injured-persons/))

## Start with detours, then estimate congestion

The recommended first engine runs shortest or fastest paths on directed edges. A full closure removes an edge from permitted routing for its active interval; an inaccessible destination returns “no legal route.” Compare the same trip and departure assumptions before and after closure, reporting additional distance and estimated travel time. Speeds must carry measured or assumed status. This stage establishes usable network behavior without claiming that rerouted vehicles have already formed queues.

Next, introduce time-binned origin–destination demand or boundary gateway flows. Allocate trips among alternative paths and update travel costs using a transparent volume–capacity function. Run baseline and disruption with identical background demand. This can show **estimated traffic pressure and comparative delay**, with low/base/high demand cases. It does not calculate physical queues or spillback: FHWA explicitly identifies that limitation of common static volume-delay curves. A browser Web Worker is a reasonable implementation choice for this small approximate model. ([FHWA modeling study](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/operations/22054/22054.pdf), [SUMO assignment methods](https://sumo.dlr.de/docs/Demand/Dynamic_User_Assignment.html))

If queue buildup, incident clearance and upstream spillback become essential, adopt **SUMO as a separate Python worker or batch service**. It supports closure intervals, rerouting and microscopic or mesoscopic simulation; its Python interface can step a native process and retrieve results for Mapbox. It still needs a cleaned network, realistic demand and calibration. Evaluate urban junction settings deliberately rather than assuming defaults model downtown intersections correctly. Precomputed demo runs are an alternative to operating a continuous worker. ([SUMO rerouters](https://sumo.dlr.de/docs/Simulation/Rerouter.html), [mesoscopic simulation](https://sumo.dlr.de/docs/Simulation/Meso.html), [Python interface](https://sumo.dlr.de/docs/TraCI/Interfacing_TraCI_from_Python.html))

Mapbox remains the renderer. Its traffic-aware Directions service can provide useful route comparisons, including beta point exclusions subject to documented limitations and response validation. It does not expose a general event-demand or lane-capacity simulation API. Traffic tiles display observed conditions; licensed raw Traffic Data is a separate product. ([Directions API](https://docs.mapbox.com/api/navigation/directions/), [Traffic tiles](https://docs.mapbox.com/data/tilesets/reference/mapbox-traffic-v1/), [Traffic Data access](https://docs.mapbox.com/data/traffic/guides/access/))

## Represent each disruption as explicit changes

A closure scenario records directed road IDs, affected lanes or vehicle classes, and start/end times. An accident records a temporary capacity or speed reduction, blocked lanes, onset and clearance assumptions, optionally with staged recovery. Lane loss does not establish a reliable proportional capacity loss: use editable severity ranges until local observations support calibration. This product simulates consequences of a specified incident; predicting accident occurrence is a separate problem. ([FHWA event handbook](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwaop04010/chapter1.htm))

An event records venue access points, attendance, arrival/departure windows, travel mode shares, car occupancy, origin distribution, parking/drop-off constraints and staging closures. Approximate arriving private vehicles as attendance multiplied by car share divided by occupancy, then distribute them through time and space. Model departures separately and account explicitly for ride-hail legs. The event calendar alone cannot fill these fields. TTC can initially identify routes and stops intersecting affected roads; passenger crowding and service recovery require further demand and operational assumptions. ([FHWA event-demand method](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwaop04010/chapter5_03.htm))

## AI should propose inputs and explain computed outputs

An LLM can turn “close this street for a 5,000-person event from 5–9 PM” into a structured draft. Resolve street names against the actual network, then validate segment IDs, direction, connectivity, times and geographic coverage in application code. Display selected roads and missing assumptions before running the engine. Structured output and function calling support this workflow, but schema correctness does not establish geographic or factual correctness. ([Structured outputs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/structured-output), [function calling](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/function-calling))

Have AI explain the engine’s returned detours, affected corridors and uncertainty. It should not invent precise delays, attendance, traffic counts or capacity factors. Custom traffic ML can wait: forecasting research does not establish causal accuracy for arbitrary unseen interventions. A useful training dataset would need aligned traffic measurements, demand context, disruption extent and timing, and before/during/after outcomes. ([DCRNN forecasting paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.01926))

## Build one auditable closure comparison next

The next work item is **“select one downtown road segment, close it, and compare a legal origin–destination route.”** Build the directed graph, verify a few representative one-way streets and grade-separated crossings, and return baseline/scenario geometry, distance, assumed travel time and any disconnected-trip warning. Keep provenance beside edge attributes. This gives all three user groups a concrete interaction before introducing uncertain congestion numbers.

Choose a modeling area with alternate corridors and gateway inflows; the camera’s Toronto boundary should not determine the simulation boundary. Validate baseline travel times and counts, then a separate observed disruption when available. Preserve observation dates and test sensitivity to demand, capacity and event mode share. The defensible hackathon claim is an explainable comparison under stated assumptions, with a clear path toward calibration. ([FHWA calibration and validation framework](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop13015/sec4.htm))
